# Name and brand

## Recommendation

Keep **Smart Bin Recognition** as the technical project and repository name.
Use **SortAtlas** as the provisional public product name while it passes user,
domain and trade-mark clearance.

```text
Product: SortAtlas
English descriptor: Local guidance for the bin in front of you
German descriptor: Tonne erkennen. Lokal richtig entsorgen.
```

This is a working decision, not legal clearance and not an instruction to rename
published identifiers yet.

## Why not WhichBin

Reject **WhichBin** and close variants.

- "Which bin?" is generic category language used by council apps, sorting
  guides and product features across several markets.
- It can mean either "which bin is collected this week?" or "where does this
  item go?" Neither describes physical-bin recognition precisely.
- Exact and near-exact variants are hard to search for and weak to own.
- It adds another `bin` name to a market already crowded with smart-bin hardware,
  collection apps and AI waste scanners.

It can remain interface copy—"Which bin is this?"—but not the brand.

## Why SortAtlas

- **Sort** states the user outcome without claiming to recognise the item.
- **Atlas** expresses local variation, geography and an expanding set of verified
  region packs.
- The name works for the PWA, data layer and future integration module.
- It is pronounceable in German and English and does not depend on a bin colour.
- A preliminary exact-name screen on 2026-08-20 found no material waste app,
  app-store listing or GitHub repository under `SortAtlas`; queried `.com`,
  `.eu`, `.de` and `.app` names returned no DNS record at that time.

The screen is encouraging, not sufficient. No DNS record does not prove a domain
is registrable, and an exact-name search does not find confusingly similar
trade marks.

## Risks in SortAtlas

- `Sort` is English and may be less immediate for some German users.
- `Atlas` may suggest a map or complete geographic coverage before either exists.
- The compound is descriptive enough that legal distinctiveness may be limited.

The descriptor must explain the product, and user testing must check meaning,
pronunciation and recall across languages.

## Name architecture

| Layer | Name | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Repository / research project | Smart Bin Recognition | retain until a public-brand decision is final |
| Public product | SortAtlas | provisional |
| Capability | bin recognition + verified local rules | descriptive, not branded as a separate AI |
| Region data | region pack | keep the existing technical term |

Do not rename permanent stream IDs, form-factor IDs, packages, datasets or model
artefacts as part of a consumer-brand change.

## Message hierarchy

Lead with the job, not the technology.

### English

```text
SortAtlas
See the bin. Get the local rule.
In your language, backed by the responsible authority.
```

### German

```text
SortAtlas
Tonne erkennen. Lokal richtig entsorgen.
In deiner Sprache, mit Quelle der zuständigen Stelle.
```

For an institutional audience:

> A verified, multilingual recognition layer for physical bins—designed to
> complement existing municipal guidance, not replace it.

Avoid "smart city platform," "revolutionising recycling," "AI-powered
sustainability," and unmeasured contamination or CO2 claims.

## Alternatives kept in reserve

| Name | Strength | Problem |
|---|---|---|
| BinCompass | clear guidance metaphor | generic `bin` category and weaker local-data meaning |
| Tonnenklar | natural German wordplay | poor international fit and tied to German vocabulary |
| BinAtlas | close to the architecture | `.com` was registered and `BinAtlas` is used in another technical field |
| Smart Bin Recognition | precise project description | confused with IoT/sensor hardware and too long for a consumer product |

Do not expand the shortlist until SortAtlas fails a named test.

## Clearance gate

Before public adoption:

1. test the name with German-speaking and non-German-speaking pilot users;
2. check spelling, pronunciation, meaning, unaided recall and product expectation;
3. search exact and similar marks in DPMAregister, EUIPO/TMview and WIPO for the
   relevant software and service classes;
4. search company registers, app stores, GitHub, social handles and search
   engines;
5. confirm and acquire the required domains and handles;
6. obtain professional trade-mark advice before filing or material launch spend;
7. only then plan the public code, manifest, copy and asset rename.

The DPMA explicitly warns that exact-element searches are not similarity
searches and recommends broader national, EU and international research. See the
[name-screen evidence](../research/10-name-screen.md).

## Name test

Show the name without explanation, then ask:

- What do you think this product does?
- How would you pronounce and spell it?
- What would you search to find it again?
- Does it sound trustworthy, official, childish or technical?
- Does "Atlas" imply a map or coverage we do not have?
- After using the prototype, does the name fit the actual job?

Pass only if the name is memorable, does not promise item recognition or smart
hardware, and creates no material language problem in the first pilot cohorts.
